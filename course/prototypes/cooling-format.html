Sources and model

This presentation covers heat capture, CDU interfaces and outdoor heat rejection. Schematics describe functional paths, not installation drawings. D10 and D11 hold the expanded reference. The CoolIT views use real manufacturer photographs of a row-scale CHx2000.

Heat balance, temperature rise and approach

The steady-flow sensible-heat balance is Q̇ = ṁ cₚ ΔT. Q̇ is heat transfer rate, ṁ is mass flow, cₚ is specific heat and ΔT is the temperature change of that fluid. For volume flow V̇, mass flow is ρ V̇, where ρ is density. The model assumes steady sensible heating with constant properties and excludes pump/fan work.

The transport comparison fixes 100 kW and a 10 K rise. Air is modeled at 1.2 kg/m³ and 1.005 kJ/(kg·K); water at 1,000 kg/m³ and 4.18 kJ/(kg·K). It yields 8.29 m³/s of air versus 2.39 L/s of water. These are illustrative bulk-flow requirements, not fan or pump ratings, cold-plate performance or proof all rack heat enters the liquid path.

The interactive water example holds heat already captured by one liquid path at 100 kW and stipulates a 35°C inlet. With cₚ = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K), changing flow from 2.5 to 5 kg/s lowers the calculated coolant rise from 9.57 to 4.78 K and outlet from 44.57 to 39.78°C. This compares two ideal steady operating points; it does not predict the transient response, chip temperature or added pumping power.

The separate CDU example uses an 84 kW load, 2 kg/s in each water loop and rounded cₚ = 4.2 kJ/(kg·K). Technology supply/return is 35/45°C and facility supply/return is 30/40°C. Loop rise is 10 K; CDU approach is the 5 K difference between the two supply streams. Approach varies with duty, flow, exchanger design and condition.

The outdoor comparison retains that CDU example. Dry bulb is 35°C; wet bulb is 22°C or 28°C. Assumed dry-cooler approach is 5 K; tower approach is 3 K. The chosen wet route includes a separate tower/facility exchanger with 5 K approach, plus the CDU's 5 K. These stipulated points give technology supply of 45°C (dry), 35°C (wet, drier air) and 41°C (wet, more humid air), against a chosen 35°C limit. They are not product curves, weather observations or universal minimum approaches.

CoolIT CHx2000: what the listed ratings establish

CoolIT's product page, checked September 11, 2026, lists 2,000 kW at 5°C approach, and separately lists 2,125 L/min at 35 psi. The latter describes flow against hydraulic resistance; pressure is not a thermal capacity. The page does not establish that these are simultaneous operating conditions. Its separate 12.24 kW electrical figure is not used to calculate efficiency because matching test conditions are absent.

A CDU's exchanger transfers heat into the facility loop; its pumps circulate rack coolant, while sensors, controls and filtration maintain the required supply conditions. The CDU still needs an external heat sink. This is a commercial example, not a claim of universal market standardization or a promise that any 2 MW rack group can use it.

Front and internal photographs are embedded from CoolIT. Their configuration is not replaced by an AI-generated illustration. Numeric brochure values from older revisions are not combined with current ratings.

Dry, wet, hybrid and refrigerated rejection

A dry cooler transfers heat through a coil into air without intentional evaporation. A wet tower evaporates part of its circulating water; makeup replaces evaporation and blowdown. Site water availability, permits, local weather and operating strategy determine feasibility. No blanket claim that wet cooling is infeasible across most of the U.S. is assumed here.

Adiabatic precooling evaporates water into the entering air ahead of a dry coil. Air-cooled and water-cooled chillers classify the condenser sink, not the fluid cooling the load. The chiller diagrams use a separate hypothetical 100 kW evaporator load plus 20 kW compressor work, giving 120 kW condenser rejection. Other auxiliaries are excluded. Economizer operation can bypass refrigeration when the outside sink is cool enough; circulation still needs fans or pumps.

Cooling redundancy and reduced-power operation

Redundancy can exist within a CDU (pumps and power supplies), across CDU groups, and through facility pumps, chillers, outdoor rejection, pipe routes, electrical supplies and controls. N counts the units required at the design duty; N+1 adds one. A spare component does not duplicate its shared water, power or control dependencies. NVIDIA's DSX reference architecture explicitly describes N+1 CDU groups with shared piping. These hypothetical units are not CoolIT product ratings.

The capacity examples stipulate a 1,000 kW selected liquid heat load and 600 kW usable capacity per CDU at qualified fluid conditions. The parallel branches are independently isolatable; the surviving pipework and plant can support the needed flow. The N+1 group has three units. One isolated unit loss leaves 1,200 kW; two leave 600 kW. A shared facility-path failure removes usable capacity even with healthy CDUs.

The separate 2N example duplicates the upstream train: two CDU modules, sufficient facility water and outdoor rejection, power and controls per train. It assumes a compatible transfer of the selected load to one surviving train. The displayed capacity is max(A, B), not A+B. The shared load-side interface remains: cold plates, common manifolds or isolation faults can still defeat that load. The drawing shows heat-removal dependencies; paired supply/return pipes are condensed.

When 600 kW remains, reducing the selected liquid heat load to 500 kW leaves 100 kW of margin. At the modeled temperature/flow conditions, heat removed is min(heat load, available capacity). Any excess heats the fluid and equipment; this model does not calculate temperature or elapsed time. The reduced load is an assumed achieved operating point after coordinated IT action, not a guaranteed automatic reaction. It is not a 500 kW GPU cap or a prediction of half the application speed. CPUs, GPUs, memory, other electronics and residual air heat have to be accounted for to connect a real electrical limit to liquid heat.

A configured power cap limits power; local thermal protection reacts to measured chip temperature and may reduce clocks or shut hardware down. Dell's CPWR0139 event explicitly documents power throttling or shutdown triggered by a liquid-cooling alert. Such coordination needs supported equipment, sensors, control authority and a working response path. Losing the final facility path leaves no sustained cooling in this model even at the lower load; leaks may instead require isolation or shutdown. Thermal storage and other heat paths, where present, need separate analysis. No transfer time, recovery time or safe delay is claimed here.

Why this particular architecture

The two fluids stay separated by the CDU heat exchanger in normal operation. The cold plate contacts the processor package through a thermal interface; coolant is inside the cold plate, not on the silicon junction. A dry cooler transfers heat from facility fluid through a coil into outdoor air. Its operating envelope depends on ambient and coolant temperatures. Other designs use refrigeration, evaporative rejection or additional loops.
