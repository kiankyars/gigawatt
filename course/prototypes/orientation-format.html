DATA CENTER OVERVIEW

Data center overview

Thirteen scenes tour generation, transmission, campus power, backup, racks, networks and cooling before comparing rating, demand and PUE. Product requirements and original operating assumptions are labeled separately.

Definitions and model boundaries

Rating, capacity and load: a nameplate states a component's rated capability under conditions. IT capacity concerns supportable IT service at a defined boundary. IT load and facility load are actual demand. Facility load includes IT and its supporting systems. These labels are not interchangeable.

GB300: NVIDIA quotes a full-rack requirement up to 142 kW, without specifying an exact metering plane in that bullet. Our ten-rack ledger separately assumes 142 kW at each AC inlet, 80 kW of additional IT, and 300 kW of facility overhead. No PSU-loss allocation or observed workload consumption is inferred. The 2,000 kW supply nameplate is hypothetical.

Demand: energy = average power × duration. The evaluation batch queue contributes 48 MWh and the fixed live-inference/support load contributes 96 MWh. Both schedules use 144 MWh. The batch queue is ready at the start and has a 24-hour deadline. Independent batches can be staggered; spare capacity and unchanged batch energy are assumptions, not a server benchmark. Minute-scale measurement effects remain in the written reference.

PUE: total facility energy ÷ IT energy over matching intervals. Same 1,500 kWh IT energy; facility energy falls 1,800→1,650 kWh; PUE falls 1.20→1.10. The same useful workload is assumed. No annual rating or reduction from a named product is claimed.

Primary sources and reading limits · checked 12 September 2026
  • Google Data Centers — Photo gallery, Central Ohio: the New Albany server-aisle photograph provides a real view of racks, aisles and overhead infrastructure. This is one facility, not a universal layout or a GB300 installation. The selectable floor plan is a separate teaching schematic, not a plan of this building.
  • DOE / NREL — Best Practices Guide for Energy-Efficient Data Center Design: executive summary and sections 2.2, 2.4, 2.5, 7.1 and 8.1 support system boundaries, internal server consumption and heat recovery. Historical equipment-efficiency figures are not used in these examples.
  • EIA — Measuring electricity: power and energy units. The traces and supply limits are original examples.
  • Leviton — White Space and Gray Space: area definitions only; no universal equipment placement or connector configuration is inferred.
  • NVIDIA — NVL72 system hardware and components: component hierarchy and electrical, thermal and network interfaces and published full-rack requirement up to 142 kW. The rack image is NVIDIA’s; the operating ledger is hypothetical.
  • The Green Grid — PUE: A Comprehensive Examination of the Metric: printed pages 8–9, 14–22 and 34 distinguish infrastructure efficiency, IT energy and productivity. Used as metric explanation, not a claim of compliance with a current standard.
  • NVIDIA hardware guide — Power shelves: AC-to-DC power shelves, nominal 50–51 V in-rack busbar and the manufacturer’s annotated DGX GB300 rear-view figure. The combined-guide consumption example is not used as a GB300 rating.
  • Google Cloud — Best practices for batch inference: scheduled jobs, latency tolerance and job concurrency. The LLM evaluation queue and MW schedule are original assumptions, not Google telemetry.
  • EIA — How electricity is generated: generator, turbine and photovoltaic mechanisms; historical generation shares are not used.
  • EIA — Delivery to consumers: interconnected grid, transmission, distribution and transformer roles. Campus voltages remain unspecified.
  • Vertiv — Deploying Liquid Cooling, pages 14–15: explicitly discusses white-space versus gray-space mechanical-gallery placement and service access. Pages 9–10 explain separate liquid circuits and remaining air cooling. No universal location or heat split is assumed.
  • Schneider Electric — Presence of a UPS: UPS support across the generator-start interval. The opening depicts one online-UPS architecture, not a switching procedure.
  • Corning — Meet-me room and outside plant: physical entry and carrier interconnection. The generic network preview is not a specified cable design.
