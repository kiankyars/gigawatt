## Opening journey

**One system journey, six engineering problems**

What has to remain true across source branches, grid delivery, campus and building power, processor switching, and heat rejection?

The course follows an engineered energy-service chain through six changes of scale. It does not trace one electron or claim that Abilene's grid and onsite sources form one evidenced as-built path. ## Closing synthesis

**Re-read the completed journey**

The engineered service chain can now be inspected through three system-level questions without pretending that every branch is one traced physical path or inventing an additional physical phase.

### Where does delivery actually bind?

Which evidenced physical, interconnection, construction, equipment, or operating gate currently limits the usable path?

- **Phase 1 · Generate:** Ask whether the selected source has crossed permit, installation, commissioning, and measured-output gates at an electrical interface.
- **Phase 2 · Transmit:** Ask whether interconnection, voltage transformation, transfer equipment, and energized grid service establish deliverable power rather than a nameplate total.
- **Phase 3 · Campus:** Ask whether independent source and feeder branches, protective interruption, and isolation conditions actually preserve service after a disturbance.
- **Phase 4 · Building:** Ask whether building equipment and A/B paths can deliver facility AC to the particular rack, including compatible load interfaces and maintenance isolation.
- **Phase 5 · Compute:** Ask whether rack and board conversion, installed accelerators, workload activity, and measured operating demand turn facility power into useful work.
- **Phase 6 · Reject heat:** Ask whether component, liquid, residual-air, facility-loop, and atmospheric-rejection boundaries can remove heat under actual operating conditions.

### Who owns the handoff and its risk?

Who builds, owns, finances, operates, and bears utilization risk at each boundary?

- **Phase 1 · Generate:** Separate generator owner and operator, fuel supply, storage, standby, and contractual-attribute roles before assigning physical delivery.
- **Phase 2 · Transmit:** Separate generator, transmission owner and operator, grid operator, interconnection customer, and site roles at each electrical boundary.
- **Phase 3 · Campus:** Separate utility service, campus sources, campus distribution, building feeder, and protective-device ownership; keep the source merge unknown where evidence is absent.
- **Phase 4 · Building:** Separate campus-feeder, building power-train, maintenance, and IT load-interface responsibility.
- **Phase 5 · Compute:** Separate facilities power, rack hardware, platform operation, and workload ownership before assigning output or utilization risk.
- **Phase 6 · Reject heat:** Separate IT cooling loop, facility loop, air-side cooling, heat-rejection plant, and water-account responsibility.

### How much capacity becomes useful compute?

Which measured inputs are required before announced power or hardware can become an evidence-backed useful-compute estimate?

- **Phase 1 · Generate:** Generation MW is only a source-side rating; it is not delivered IT power, accelerators, workload activity, or useful output.
- **Phase 2 · Transmit:** Grid ratings and interconnection capacity are not current site demand or rack power.
- **Phase 3 · Campus:** Campus service and feeder capability are not the count of energized buildings, racks, accelerators, or live workloads.
- **Phase 4 · Building:** Power at a rack position is not board input, processor-rail power, active silicon, or useful workload output.
- **Phase 5 · Compute:** An estimate requires measured electrical inputs, installed hardware, workload configuration, and observed throughput or utilization on the same basis.
- **Phase 6 · Reject heat:** Cooling capacity and design posture constrain availability but do not quantify useful compute; use measured operating power, thermal state, and output.
