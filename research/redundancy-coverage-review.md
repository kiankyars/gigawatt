# UPS redundancy coverage review

Reviewed **2026-09-08**. Research question: **Does GIGAWATT teach a learner to distinguish N, N+1, N+2, 2N and 2(N+1), and test the promised service through a failure and maintenance?**

**Verdict: not yet sufficiently comprehensive for that outcome.** D05 already develops N+1 capacity arithmetic, maintenance followed by another module loss, the difference between capacity and path independence, and electrical/thermal continuity. Those are useful foundations. N+2 and 2(N+1) are absent from the authored expansion; 2N receives a conceptual comparison without a worked module/path comparison or a changed-case redundancy exercise. UPS bypass paths and single-corded load interfaces are material omissions. Add one bounded redundancy sequence before treating D05.3 as taught and assessed.

This is a coverage audit and teaching proposal, not a course rewrite, equipment design or certification assessment. The lesson sources and baseline diagram code were inspected; no new UPS presentation was built or visually validated. Only the separate AC/DC sample currently has a complete dedicated presentation sequence, as [PRESENTING.md](../course/PRESENTING.md) states.

## Original worked comparison: 6 MW load, 2 MW modules

Define the boundary first: a constant **6 MW total protected load** and identical hypothetical UPS units, each delivering **2 MW continuous real output** under the declared operating conditions. Thus **N = ceiling(6/2) = 3 modules**. A module here is an entire capacity unit; internal power modules, battery strings and phase conductors are different counting boundaries. Three-phase equipment is not intrinsically three independent redundant paths.

For N, N+1 and N+2, place the units in **one parallel bank feeding one output bus**. For 2N, place three units on A and three on B; for 2(N+1), place four on A and four on B. A and B are separate complete routes to suitably dual-fed loads. Their independence and load acceptance are explicit scenario assumptions, not consequences of the module count.

Assume each unavailable module is isolated without disturbing surviving units; all remaining units can deliver their full stated output; no common bus, control, battery, source or cooling dependency fails. Do not credit temporary overload. For the two-route cases, use a deliberately conservative screen: **at least one complete surviving route must be able to supply all 6 MW**. This tests retained full-route capability, not every possible normal load-sharing state.

| Arrangement | Installed output rating | Isolated module outages that preserve the stated capacity screen | Maintenance followed by another event |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| N: 3 in one bank | 6 MW | None: one unavailable leaves 4 MW. | Removing one module already fails the 6 MW screen. |
| N+1: 4 in one bank | 8 MW | Any one leaves 6 MW; two leave 4 MW. | One maintained plus one failed leaves 4 MW. |
| N+2: 5 in one bank | 10 MW | Any two leave 6 MW; three leave 4 MW. | One maintained plus one failed leaves 6 MW. The common output bus still has no alternate route. |
| 2N: A has 3, B has 3 | 12 MW | Any single module outage leaves the other complete 6 MW route. Losing all three modules on one route also leaves 6 MW on the other. Two outages split one per route leave 4 MW on each, so no single route meets this screen. | All of A unavailable leaves B at 6 MW; one subsequent B module outage leaves only 4 MW. |
| 2(N+1): A has 4, B has 4 | 16 MW | Any three isolated module outages leave at least one route with three units, hence 6 MW. Four outages split two per route leave 4 MW on each; four on one route leave 8 MW on the other. | All of A unavailable leaves B at 8 MW; one subsequent B module outage still leaves 6 MW. |

The 4+4 MW two-route state **does not prove service has stopped**. Suitable dual-cord loads may continue sharing the 6 MW across both remaining routes. It fails the stronger requirement that either surviving route alone can support the whole load. Document actual load partitioning, PSU behavior and transfer capability before claiming either continuity or interruption. The “any three” result above is conditional arithmetic for these specific four-unit banks, not a generic certification claim about 2(N+1).

**N+2 adds two capacity units within a bank; 2N provides two full-capacity systems at a stated boundary.** More total MW does not determine which faults are survived. A common output-bus fault defeats the illustrated N+2 bank regardless of its two spare modules. A genuinely independent B route can survive loss of A, subject to the load and transition requirements. Conversely, a shared upstream, bypass or control dependency can invalidate that independence. Schneider’s [White Paper 75](https://www.se.com/us/en/download/document/SPD_SADE-5TPL8X_EN/) distinguishes the common-bus parallel arrangement from system-plus-system configurations (pp. 8–9, 15–17).

Counting modules proves none of the following: actual fault containment; battery power/energy in the degraded configuration; source availability; bypass ratings and independence; selective protection in utility, generator and inverter states; post-transfer loading; cooling continuity; maintainable isolation; or integrated acceptance. Do not translate these shorthand labels into an Uptime Tier or an uptime percentage. Uptime explicitly rejects component-count-to-Tier equivalence in its [Tier misconceptions article](https://journal.uptimeinstitute.com/myths-and-misconceptions-regarding-the-uptime-institutes-tier-certification-system/).

## Prioritized teaching plan

1. **P0 — Make module capacity and path independence separate visible questions.** Extend D05 paths with the five configurations above, one common load boundary and explicit module-versus-system definitions. Ask what remains after a named outage, including maintenance first. Teach N+2 versus 2N before adding further topology labels; avoid expanding into every distributed-redundant variant.
2. **P0 — Draw one UPS and its bypass paths.** Introduce rectifier, DC link/battery, inverter and static bypass, then show maintenance bypass as the route intended to permit UPS isolation. Trace the supported load through each declared mode. Bypass supply can keep a load energized without supplying the same conditioning or battery-backed continuity. Requested static bypass, forced static bypass and maintenance bypass need separate behavior descriptions; the [Schneider mode reference](https://productinfo.se.com/easyups3pmodular/990-6537-easy-ups-3-phase-modular-50-250-kw-operation/English/990-6537%20Operation%20Easy%20UPS%203-Phase%20Modular%2050-250%20kW_0001015104.xml/%24/GalaxyPX_UPSModes_0000761714) demonstrates why “bypass” alone is insufficient. Show which buses, battery arrangements, controls and bypass components are shared. Distinguish a UPS static bypass from a static transfer switch (STS) selecting between two supplies. Use conceptual state diagrams, not switching instructions.
3. **P0 — Finish the path at the rack.** Connect independent A/B distribution to correctly grouped PSU inputs that can support the full required load on one feed. Add one single-corded load through a stated compatible transfer arrangement, exposing its downstream common point. Compare normal loading with surviving-feed loading; no generic transfer time, no-break guarantee or overload allowance. Require the chosen equipment’s source-acceptance, synchronization, transfer and overload evidence. Schneider’s [dual-cord explanation](https://blog.se.com/datacenter/architecture/2014/08/06/two-cords-guarantee-power-redundancy-device/) identifies both nonredundant PSU groupings and changed demand after a feed loss.
4. **P1 — Join D05 to existing cooling/control and commissioning lessons.** Add the pump/control dependencies from D12/C01 to the same drawing. Build a compact acceptance matrix: starting configuration, unavailable element, expected supported load, transient acceptance limits, observations and restoration/recharge readiness. Include normal operation, module fault, module maintenance plus fault, whole-route maintenance, bypass state and common-dependency loss. Ask for configuration-specific records and unresolved limits before accepting the continuity claim.

## Bounded presenter sequence and learner exercise

Build **one eight-beat D05 sequence, approximately 10–12 minutes plus practice**, with separate speaker notes and reading explanation. This is a proposed rehearsal target, not a recorded duration. Reuse the existing A/B graphic language; author the equipment and bus relationships explicitly.

| Beat | Visible change and question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | One UPS cutaway: which conversion/storage/bypass path is carrying the protected load? |
| 2 | Three 2 MW blocks serve 6 MW: what does N count? |
| 3 | Add a fourth block; remove one, then another: what does maintenance consume? |
| 4 | Add the fifth block on the same bus: what does N+2 fix, and what does a bus fault still remove? |
| 5 | Replace the bank with independent three-unit A/B routes; disable A: how is 2N different? |
| 6 | Add one unit per route; maintain all of A, then lose one unit in B: what does 2(N+1) preserve? |
| 7 | Reveal bypass, batteries, controls, A/B PSU groups and one single-corded load: where does independence end? |
| 8 | Add cooling/control availability tracks and a short acceptance record: which continuity claim is supported, and which evidence is missing? |

**Changed-case exercise.** The protected load rises to **7 MW** while module ratings and installed counts remain unchanged. Give the learner the one-line diagrams, and ask them to recalculate N, classify each arrangement relative to the new load, and test one maintained module plus another isolated module outage in the single bank. Then maintain all of A and remove one B module in each two-route design. Finally identify a common controller or bypass dependency that module arithmetic cannot resolve.

**Answer key.** N becomes ceiling(7/2) = 4. Three units are undersized; four are N; five are N+1, and five minus two leaves 6 MW, short of 7 MW. The former three-plus-three 2N design is no longer 2N for this load because neither route can carry 7 MW. Four-plus-four becomes 2N, rather than 2(N+1); with A unavailable and one B module lost, B supplies only 6 MW. Supplying 2(N+1) at 7 MW would require five of these units per route under the stated assumptions. Aggregate MW across two inadequate routes may support a particular sharing state, but does not establish full-route redundancy. A shared controller or bypass requires a topology and behavior investigation, not another arithmetic label.

Completion requires the learner to explain **which event, which capacity boundary, which surviving route and which unproved dependency**. A correct shorthand label alone is insufficient. The capacity/outage cases in this note were checked by enumerating module removals; that verifies the stated arithmetic only.
